\section{Functors}
--- Let $\Cat$ and $\mathbf{D}$ be two categories. A \emph{functor} $F$ from
$\Cat$ into $\mathbf{D}$ (we write $F\colon \Cat\to\mathbf{D}$) is constituted
by the following data:

\begin{itemize}
\item A map $M \leadsto FM$ of $\mathbf{OC}$ into $\mathbf{OD}$.
\item For every pair $(M,N)$ of objects of $\Cat$, we are given a map
      $F(M,N)$ of $\operatorname{Hom}_{\Cat}(M,N)$ into
      $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{D}}(FM,FN)$. [We shall also write $F$
      instead of $F(M,N)$.]
\end{itemize}

We also suppose that $(Ff)\circ(Fg)$ is equal to $F(f\circ g)$ whenever the
source of $f$ coincides with the target of $g$.

For example, if $\Cat$ is a full subcategory of $\mathbf{D}$, we call
\emph{canonical functor} of $\Cat$ into $\mathbf{D}$ the functor $F$ which is
defined by the following equalities: $FM = M$ if $M\in\mathbf{OC}$;
$Ff = f$ if $f\in\mathbf{MC}$. In the case where $\Cat$ coincides with
$\mathbf{D}$, we shall also say that $F$ is the \emph{identity functor} of
$\Cat$. We shall then write $I_{\Cat}$ instead of $F$.

The reader will note that, with the terminology of \cite{grothendieck-1}, we consider here only
covariant functors. When no confusion will be possible, it may happen to us not
to make explicit the maps $F(M,N)$, but only the map of $\mathbf{OC}$ into
$\mathbf{OD}$ which is associated with $F$. The symbol $M\leadsto FM$ shall
then designate the functor $F$.

The functors of $\Cat$ into $\mathbf{D}$ are the objects of a category which we
shall denote $\operatorname{Hom}(\Cat,\mathbf{D})$ and whose morphisms are
defined as follows: let $F$ and $G$ be two functors of $\Cat$ into $\mathbf{D}$;
a morphism (or \emph{functorial morphism} --- French \emph{morphisme
functoriel} --- that is, a natural transformation) $\varphi$ of $F$ into $G$
consists in the datum, for every object $M$ of $\Cat$, of a morphism
$\varphi(M)\colon FM\to GM$. We suppose in addition that, for every morphism
$f\colon M\to N$ of $\Cat$, the following diagram is commutative:

\[
\begin{tikzcd}
FM \arrow[r, "\varphi^{(M)}"] \arrow[d, "Ff"'] & GM \arrow[d, "Gf"] \\
FN \arrow[r, "\varphi^{(N)}"'] & GN
\end{tikzcd}
\]

If $\varphi\colon F\to G$ and $\psi\colon G\to H$ are two natural
transformations, the composite $\psi\circ\varphi$ is defined by the formula
\[
  (\psi\circ\varphi)(M) = \psi(M)\circ_{\mathbf{D}}\varphi(M).
\]

It follows in particular that $\varphi$ is an isomorphism in
$\operatorname{Hom}(\Cat,\mathbf{D})$ if and only if $\varphi(M)$ is an
isomorphism for every object $M$ of $\Cat$; we then say that $\varphi$ is
\emph{a functorial isomorphism}.

Let us now consider two objects $X$ and $Y$ of $\Cat$; since we suppose that
$\Cat$ is a $\Univ$-category, $\operatorname{Hom}_{\Cat}(X,Y)$ is an element of
$\Univ$. We denote by $\dot X$, $\operatorname{Hom}_{\Cat}(X,.)$ or
$\operatorname{Hom}(X,.)$ the functor $Y\leadsto\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)$
of $\Cat$ into $\Ens$. If $f\colon X\to X'$ is a morphism of $\Cat$, we denote
by $\dot f$ or by $\operatorname{Hom}(f,.)$ the natural transformation which
applies the element $g$ of $\dot X' Y = \operatorname{Hom}(X',Y)$ to the
element $g\circ f$ of $\dot X Y = \operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)$.

The maps $X\leadsto\dot X$ and $f\leadsto\dot f$ obviously define a functor of
$\Cat^{\circ}$ into $\operatorname{Hom}(\Cat,\Ens)$.\ednote{The original
prints $\operatorname{Hom}(\Cat,\Ab)$ here; the values
$\dot XY = \operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)$ are sets, so we print
$\operatorname{Hom}(\Cat,\Ens)$.} We are going to examine
this functor a little more closely: for this, let $F$ be an arbitrary functor
of $\Cat$ into $\Ab$; every element $\xi$ of $FX$ then defines a natural
transformation $\dot\xi$ of $\dot X$ into $F$: for every object $Y$ of $\Cat$,
$\dot\xi(Y)$ is the map $f\leadsto(Ff)(\xi)$ of
$\dot X Y = \operatorname{Hom}(X,Y)$ into $FY$.

\begin{proposition}
For every object $X$ of $\Cat$ the map $\xi\leadsto\dot\xi$ of $FX$ into
$\operatorname{Hom}(\dot X,F)$ is bijective.
\end{proposition}

In fact, let $\varphi$ be a natural transformation of $\dot X$ into $F$; let
$\dot\varphi$ be the image of $1_X$ under the map $\varphi(X)$ of
$\operatorname{Hom}(X,X)$ into $FX$. In the case where
$\varphi$ is equal to $\dot\xi$, $\dot\varphi$ is none other than $\xi$. It
remains therefore to prove that $\varphi = \dot\xi$ if
$\xi = \dot\varphi$. In other words, we must show that for every object $Y$ of
$\Cat$ and every morphism $f\colon X\to Y$ we have the equality
\[
  \varphi(Y)(f) = (Ff)(\xi).
\]

This follows from the commutative diagram
% The label of the left arrow needs its comma braced: tikz-cd splits the
% arrow options on top-level commas before it sees the quotes.
\[
\begin{tikzcd}
\operatorname{Hom}(X,Y) \arrow[r, "\varphi(Y)"] & FY \\
\operatorname{Hom}(X,X) \arrow[r, "\varphi(X)"]
  \arrow[u, "\operatorname{Hom}(X{,}f)"] & FX \arrow[u, "Ff"']
\end{tikzcd}
\]
and from the fact that $\dot\xi = \varphi(X)(1_X)$.

\begin{corollary}[\cite{grothendieck-2}]
If $X$ and $X'$ are two objects of $\Cat$, the map
$f\leadsto\operatorname{Hom}(f,.)$ of $\operatorname{Hom}(X',X)$ into the set
of the natural transformations of $\operatorname{Hom}(X,.)$ into
$\operatorname{Hom}(X',.)$ is bijective.
\end{corollary}

The corollary is proved by replacing $F$ by $\dot X'$ in Proposition~1.

\begin{corollary}
Every isomorphism of the functor $\operatorname{Hom}(X,.)$ onto the functor
$\operatorname{Hom}(X',.)$ is induced by an isomorphism of $X'$ onto $X$.
\end{corollary}

We shall henceforth say that a functor $F$ of $\Cat$ into $\Ens$ is
\emph{representable} \cite{grothendieck-2} if $F$ is isomorphic to a functor
$\operatorname{Hom}(X,.)$. We also say that $X$ is \emph{a
representative} of $F$. Such a representative is obviously defined up to an
isomorphism.

In particular, if the functor $F$ which associates to every object the set
$\{\emptyset\}$ is representable, we choose once and for all a representative
$0$ of $F$. This representative will be called \emph{the initial object} of
$\Cat$. For every object $Y$ of $\Cat$, we then denote by $\eta_Y$ the unique
morphism of $0$ into $Y$.

Dually, \emph{the final object} $0'$ of $\Cat$, if it exists, is such that
$\operatorname{Hom}(Y,0')$ contains one and only one element $\varepsilon_Y$
for every object $Y$.

We say that $0$ is \emph{zero} if it is both initial and final. If $M$ and
$N$ are then two objects of $\Cat$, we denote by $0_{M,N}$ or simply $0$ in
place of $\eta_N\circ\varepsilon_M$.\ednote{The original prints
$O_{M,N}$ (and $\eta_M\circ\varepsilon_N$), with the indices transposed;
we print $0_{M,N}$ (and $\eta_N\circ\varepsilon_M$), the composite
$M\to 0\to N$. The $O/o$ itself is rendered $0$ throughout, per the
house convention.}

Let us give another example: we recall that a \emph{direct sum} of two objects
$M$ and $N$ is a triple $(P,u,v)$ made up of an object $P$ and of two
morphisms, $u\colon M\to P$ and $v\colon N\to P$; moreover, we suppose that
the maps $\operatorname{Hom}(u,X)$ and $\operatorname{Hom}(v,X)$ define for
every object $X$ an isomorphism of $\operatorname{Hom}(P,X)$ onto the
Cartesian product
$\operatorname{Hom}(M,X)\times\operatorname{Hom}(N,X)$. The object $P$ is
therefore a representative of the functor
\[
  X \leadsto \operatorname{Hom}(M,X)\times\operatorname{Hom}(N,X).
\]

If there exist direct sums of $M$ and $N$, we choose one of them which we
denote by $(M\boldsymbol{\Sigma}N,\, j_M,\, j'_N)$. We then say that
$M\boldsymbol{\Sigma}N$ is \emph{the} direct sum of $M$ and $N$ and that
$j_M$ and $j'_N$ are \emph{the} canonical morphisms.

The dual notion is the notion of direct product of $M$ and $N$: if it exists,
we denote \emph{the} product $M\boldsymbol{\Pi}N$; \emph{the} canonical
morphisms are denoted $q_M$ and $q'_N$.\ednote{The original prints $q'_M$
here; the parallel notations $(M\boldsymbol{\Sigma}N,j_M,j'_N)$ above and
$(B\boldsymbol{\Pi}C,q_B,q'_C)$ on p.~333, confirmed by Lemma~1's
$q'_N\circ h(M,N)$ on the same page, show the second projection belongs to
$N$. We print $q'_N$.}

Let us now consider the diagrams (1) and (2):

\[
\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=2.6em, column sep=3.4em]
& B \\
(1)\quad A \arrow[ur, "h"] \arrow[dr, "k"'] & \\
& C
\end{tikzcd}
\qquad\qquad\qquad
\begin{tikzcd}[row sep=2.6em, column sep=3.4em]
& B \arrow[dl, "f"] \\
(2)\quad A & \\
& C \arrow[ul, "g"']
\end{tikzcd}
\]

A \emph{fibred sum} (\emph{somme fibrée}) of the diagram (1) is a triple
$(P,u,v)$ made up of an object $P$ and of two morphisms, $u\colon B\to P$,
$v\colon C\to P$, such that we have: $u\circ h = v\circ k$; for every pair
$(b,c)$ of morphisms such that $b\circ h$ and $c\circ k$ are defined and
equal, there exists one and only one morphism $a$ whose source is $P$, which
has the same target as $b$ and $c$ and which satisfies the equalities
$b = a\circ u$, $c = a\circ v$. If there exist fibred sums of the diagram (1),
we choose one of them which we denote by
\[
  (B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_A C,\, j_B,\, j'_C).
\]
We then say that $B\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_A C$ is \emph{the} fibred sum of the
diagram (1) and that $j_B$ and $j'_C$ are \emph{the} canonical morphisms.

The dual notion is that of \emph{fibred product}: if it exists, we denote
\emph{the} fibred product of the diagram (2) by $B\boldsymbol{\Pi}_A C$;
\emph{the} canonical morphisms are denoted $q_B$ and $q'_C$.
