\section{Injective Envelopes in Abelian Categories [8]}
--- Let $\Cat$ be an abelian category and let $M$ be an object of $\Cat$.
An \emph{essential extension} of $M$ is a monomorphism
$i\colon M\to P$ satisfying the following condition: if $f$ is a
morphism with source $P$ and if $f\circ i$ is a monomorphism, then $f$
is a monomorphism.

It amounts to the same to say that a subobject $Q$ of $P$ is zero if
$Q\cap i(M)$ is zero. If $M$ is a subobject of $P$, and if $i$ is the
canonical monomorphism of $M$ into $P$, we shall say that $P$ is an
essential extension of $M$.

\begin{lemma}
Let $i$ and $j$ be two monomorphisms, $i\colon M\to P$, $j\colon P\to
Q$. Then $j\circ i$ is an essential extension of $M$ if and only if
$j$ is an essential extension of $P$ and $i$ an essential extension of
$M$.
\end{lemma}

Suppose in fact that $j\circ i$ is an essential extension of $M$: if
$f$ is a monomorphism with source $Q$ and if $f\circ j$ is a
monomorphism, then $f\circ i$ is a monomorphism; from this we deduce
that $f$ is a monomorphism and that $j$ is an essential extension of
$P$.

Let now $R$ be a subobject of $P$ such that $R\cap i(M) = 0$; let $f$
be the canonical morphism from $Q$ onto $Q/j(R)$. Under these
conditions, $f\circ j\circ i$ is a monomorphism and $R$ is zero. This
shows that $i$ is an essential extension of $M$.

The converse is clear.

\begin{lemma}
Let $i\colon M\to P$, $j\colon N\to Q$ be two essential extensions. The
morphism $i\oplus j'\colon M\oplus N\to P\oplus Q$ is an essential
extension of $M\oplus N$.
\end{lemma}

We may suppose, in order to simplify, that $M$ and $N$ are subobjects
of $P$ and $Q$, with $i = i_M^P$, $j = i_N^Q$. Let $R$ be a nonzero
subobject of $P\oplus Q$. Then at least one of the objects $q_P(R)$
and $q'_Q(R)$ is not zero. If $q_P(R)$ is not zero, the same is true of
$q_P(R)\cap M$ and of $R_1 = q_P^{-1}(M)\cap R$. If $R_1$ is contained
in $q_Q^{-1}(N)$, we have shown that $R\cap (M\oplus N)$ is not zero.
In the other case, $q'_Q(R_1)$ is not zero and ``cuts'' $N$; the
subobject $R_2 = q_Q^{-1}(N)\cap R_1$ therefore cannot be zero. In every
case a nonzero subobject of $P\oplus Q$ ``cuts'' $M\oplus N$, which is
what we had to prove.

A subobject $N$ of $M$ is said to be \emph{irreducible in $M$} if $N$
is different from $M$ and if, for every pair $(P,Q)$ of subobjects of
$M$ containing $N$, the relation $P\cap Q = N$ implies $P = N$ or
$Q = N$. It amounts to the same to say that $M/N$ is an essential
extension of every nonzero subobject. If $0$ is irreducible in $M$, we
also say that $M$ is \emph{coreducible}.

Let us consider an object $M$ of $\Cat$ and $r$ subobjects
$N_1, N_2, \ldots, N_r$. Let $p_l$ be the canonical epimorphism from
$M$ onto $M/N_l$ and $q_l$ the canonical projection of
$M/N_1\oplus M/N_2\oplus\cdots\oplus M/N_r$ onto the $l$th factor
$M/N_l$. There exists a morphism $u$ from $M$ into
$M/N_1\oplus\cdots\oplus M/N_r$, and only one, such that $q_l\circ u$
is equal to $p_l$ for every $l$.

\begin{lemma}
If the subobjects $N_l$ are irreducible in $M$, if $0$ is the
intersection of the $N_l$ and is not the intersection of fewer than $r$
of the $N_l$, then $u$ is an essential extension of $M$.
\end{lemma}

Let in fact $P_l$ be the intersection of the $N_m$ for $m \neq l$. The
morphisms $q_m\circ u$ ``annihilate'' $P_l$ if $m \neq l$, and
$q_l\circ u$ induces a monomorphism $u_l$ of $P_l$ into $M/N_l$. The
canonical monomorphisms of $P_l$ into $M$ induce a morphism $v$ from
the direct sum $\bigoplus_l P_l$ into $M$; moreover, it is clear that
$u\circ v$ is nothing but the morphism $\bigoplus_l u_l$ of
$\bigoplus_l P_l$ into $\bigoplus_l M/N_l$. Since $u_l$ is an essential
extension of $P_l$ for every $l$, the preceding lemmas show that
$\bigoplus_l u_l$, $u$ and $v$ are essential extensions.

If $I$ is an injective object of $\Cat$, every monomorphism
$u\colon I\to P$ induces an isomorphism of $I$ onto a direct factor of
$P$. If $Q$ is a complement of $u(I)$, the intersection $Q\cap u(I)$ is
zero. It follows that $u$ can be an essential extension of $I$ only if
$u$ is an isomorphism. We shall henceforth say that a monomorphism
$i\colon M\to I$ is an \emph{injective envelope} of $M$ if $i$ is an
essential extension of $M$ and if $I$ is an injective object of $\Cat$.

\begin{proposition}
Let $i\colon M\to I$ be an injective envelope of $M$ and let
$j\colon M\to J$ be a monomorphism. The following conditions are
equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[a.] The morphism $j$ is an injective envelope of $M$.
\item[b.] The morphism $j$ is an essential extension of $M$ and every
      essential extension of $J$ is an isomorphism.
\item[c.] The object $J$ is injective; moreover, for every monomorphism
      $h$ of $M$ into an injective $H$ and every morphism $l$ of $H$
      into $J$ such that $l\circ h = j$, $l$ is an epimorphism.
\end{enumerate}
If these conditions are satisfied, there exists an isomorphism $u$ of
$J$ onto $I$ such that $u\circ j = i$.
\end{proposition}

The implication $(a)\Rightarrow(b)$ is clear. Let us prove that $(b)$
implies $(a)$: since $I$ is injective, there exists a morphism $u$ of
$J$ into $I$ such that $u\circ j = i$; since $j$ is an essential
extension, $u$ is a monomorphism, hence an essential extension by
lemma~1; it follows that $u$ is an isomorphism.

Let us prove that $(a)$ implies $(c)$: since $H$ is injective, there
exists a morphism $v$ of $I$ into $H$ such that $v\circ i = h$. The
morphism $l\circ v$ is a monomorphism of $I$ into $J$, hence an
essential extension of $I$, hence an isomorphism. It follows that $l$ is
an epimorphism.

Conversely, $(c)$ implies $(a)$: for every morphism $v$ of $I$ into $J$
such that $j = v\circ i$ is necessarily an isomorphism.

The last assertion has been proved on the way.

If $i\colon M\to I$ is an injective envelope of $M$, the preceding
proposition shows that the object $I$ is determined up to an
isomorphism. Although in general this isomorphism is not unique, we
shall sometimes say, loosely, that $I$ is \emph{the} injective envelope
of $M$. If $j\colon N\to J$ is a second injective envelope, we shall
say that $M$ and $N$ have the same injective envelope if $I$ is
isomorphic to $J$.

\begin{proposition}
Let $M_1,\ldots,M_r$, $r$ objects of $\Cat$; for every integer $i$
between $1$ and $r$, let $u_i\colon M_i\to I_i$ be an injective
envelope of $M_i$. Then the morphism $\bigoplus_i u_i$ of
$\bigoplus_i M_i$ into $\bigoplus_i I_i$ is an injective envelope of
$\bigoplus_i M_i$.
\end{proposition}

In fact, every direct product of injective objects is an injective
object: this shows that $\bigoplus_i I_i$ is an injective object.
Moreover, lemma~2 shows that $\bigoplus_i u_i$ is an essential
extension.

In the following corollary we use the notations of lemma~3.

\begin{unnumberedcorollary}
Suppose that the hypotheses of lemma~3 are satisfied and let, for
every $i$, $u_i$ be an injective envelope of $M/N_i$. Then
$(\bigoplus_i u_i)\circ u$ is an injective envelope of $M$.
\end{unnumberedcorollary}

The preceding corollary discloses the connection there is between the
notion of injective envelope and the decomposition of $0$ as the
intersection of irreducible submodules in $M$.

We shall henceforth say that $\Cat$ is \emph{a category with injective
envelopes} if, for every object $M$, there exists an injective envelope
of $M$.

\begin{proposition}
The following two assertions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[a.] $\Cat$ is a category with injective envelopes.
\item[b.] For every object $M$ of $\Cat$, there exists a monomorphism
      of $M$ into an injective object; moreover, if $M$ is a subobject
      of an object $N$, there exists a subobject $Q$ of $N$ such that
      $M\cap Q$ is zero, and which is maximal among the subobjects of
      $N$ satisfying this condition.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

$(a)\Rightarrow(b)$: for if $M$ is a subobject of $N$ and if
$i\colon M\to I$ is an injective envelope of $M$, there exists a
morphism $\varphi$ of $N$ into $I$ such that $i = \varphi\circ i_M^N$.
It then suffices to put $Q = \operatorname{Ker}\varphi$.

$(b)\Rightarrow(a)$: let in fact $j$ be a monomorphism of $M$ into an
injective $J$, and let $Q$ be a subobject of $J$ which is maximal for
the equality $Q\cap j(M) = 0$. Likewise, let $I$ be a subobject of $J$,
containing $M$ and maximal for the equality $I\cap Q = 0$ (to see that
such an $I$ exists, consider the quotient $J/M$). If $p$ is the
canonical epimorphism from $J$ onto $J/Q$, then $p\circ j$ is an
essential extension of $M$ and $p$ induces an isomorphism of $I$ onto
$p(I)$; the inverse isomorphism extends to a monomorphism $h$ of
$J/Q$ into $J$; then $h(J/Q)$ is an essential extension of $I$. Hence
$I$ is equal to $h(J/Q)$; we have $I + Q = J$, $I\cap Q = 0$, and the
monomorphism of $M$ into $I$ induced by $j$ is an injective envelope of
$M$.

\begin{proposition}[17]
Let $\Cat$ be an abelian category with injective envelopes, $M$ a
nonzero object of $\Cat$ and $i\colon M\to I$ an injective envelope of
$M$. The following assertions are equivalent:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[a.] $M$ is coreducible.
\item[b.] $I$ is indecomposable.
\item[c.] $I$ is the injective envelope of every nonzero subobject of
      $I$.
\item[d.] The ring of endomorphisms of $I$ is a local ring.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

The equivalence of the assertions $(a)$, $(b)$, $(c)$ is clear. Let us
prove simply that $(b)$ and $(c)$ imply $(d)$; for if
$u\colon I\to I$ is a monomorphism, $u(I)$ is a direct factor of $I$;
assertion $(b)$ therefore implies that $u$ is an automorphism. It
suffices consequently to show that
$\operatorname{Ker}u \neq 0$ and $\operatorname{Ker}v \neq 0$ imply
$\operatorname{Ker}(u + v) \neq 0$: but
$\operatorname{Ker}u\cap\operatorname{Ker}v \neq 0$ by $(c)$ and
$\operatorname{Ker}(u + v)$ contains this intersection.

\begin{unnumberedcorollary}
Let $M$ and $N$ be two coreducible objects of a category with injective
envelopes. Then $M$ and $N$ have the same injective envelope if and only
if $N$ contains a subobject $M'$, nonzero and isomorphic to a subobject
$N'$ of $N$.
\end{unnumberedcorollary}

If $N$ is a subobject of $M$, one sometimes calls any subobject $Q$ of
$M$ which is maximal for the equality $M\cap Q = 0$ a \emph{complement
of $N$ in $M$}. If such a $Q$ exists, the complements of $Q$ which
contain $N$ are the maximal elements of the set of subobjects of $M$
which are essential extensions of $N$. This situation is often put to
good use. In particular the reader will verify that the direct factors
of an injective object $I$ coincide with the subobjects of $I$ ``which
are a complement of one of their complements''.
