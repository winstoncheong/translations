\chapter{Left Exact Functors and Injective Envelopes}
\markboth{Chapter II. Left Exact Functors and Injective Envelopes}{}

According to a result of \textsc{Grothendieck} [10], every abelian category
with generators and exact inductive limits has enough injective objects. We
begin here the study of these injective objects. We show also how one can
reduce the study of certain categories to the study of a category with
generators and exact inductive limits.

\section{Functor Categories}
--- Let $\mathbf{C}$ and $\mathbf{D}$ be two additive categories; we suppose
that the set of objects and the set of morphisms of $\mathbf{C}$ are elements
of the universe $\Univ$. Under these conditions we denote by
$\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$, and we call the
\emph{category of functors} of $\mathbf{C}$ into $\mathbf{D}$ the full
subcategory of $\operatorname{Hom}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$ whose objects are
the additive functors of $\mathbf{C}$ into $\mathbf{D}$ (cf.\
Chapter~I, \S3).

\begin{proposition}
Under the hypotheses we have made above, $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$
is an additive category. If $\mathbf{D}$ is an abelian category, it is the same
for $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$. If $\mathbf{D}$ is a category
with inductive limits (resp.\ with exact inductive limits, resp.\ with
projective limits), then $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$ is a
category with inductive limits (resp.\ with exact inductive limits, resp.\
with projective limits).
\end{proposition}

If $E$ and $F$ are two objects of $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$,
then $\operatorname{Hom}(E,F)$ is obviously a subset of the product
$\prod_{M\in\mathbf{OC}}\operatorname{Hom}(EM,FM)$. This shows that
$\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{D})$ is a $\Univ$-category. The rest
is fairly clear.

{\itshape
The rest of this paragraph is devoted to the study of
$\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$ when $\mathbf{C}$ is an
additive category whose set of objects and set of morphisms belong to $\Univ$.
We denote by $\mathbf{Z}[C]$ the following ring, which in general has no unit
element:
\par}

\begin{itemize}
\item The abelian group underlying $\mathbf{Z}[C]$ is the direct sum of the
      groups $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}}(M,N)$ as $M$ and $N$ range over
      the objects of $\mathbf{C}$.

\item Let $f\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}}(M,N)$ and
      $g\in\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}}(P,Q)$ be two elements of
      $\mathbf{Z}[C]$. The product $f.g$ is the composite morphism $f\circ g$
      when $Q$ coincides with $M$. In the contrary case $f.g$ is zero.
\end{itemize}

For every object $X$ of $\mathbf{C}$, the identity morphism
$\mathbf{\iota}_X$ of $X$ is an idempotent of $\mathbf{Z}[C]$. If $X$ and $Y$
are two distinct objects of $\mathbf{C}$, the idempotents
$\mathbf{\iota}_X$ and $\mathbf{\iota}_Y$ are orthogonal:
$\mathbf{\iota}_X.\mathbf{\iota}_Y = \mathbf{\iota}_Y.\mathbf{\iota}_X = 0$.
It follows that, for every left $\mathbf{Z}[C]$-module $M$, the element
$\mathbf{\iota}_X$ defines a projection of $M$ onto a direct factor
$\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$. Moreover, the sum of the abelian subgroups
$\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$ is direct and it is equal to $\mathbf{Z}[C].M$.

\begin{lemma}
If $N$ is a submodule of $M$ (resp.\ $Q$ a quotient of $M$) and if
$\mathbf{Z}[C].M$ is equal to $M$, then $\mathbf{Z}[C].N$ is equal to $N$
(resp.\ $\mathbf{Z}[C].Q$ is equal to $Q$).
\end{lemma}

An element $m$ of $M$ belongs in fact to $\mathbf{Z}[C].M$ if and only if
there is a finite number of objects of $\mathbf{C}$, say $X, Y, \dots, Z$,
such that
\[
  m = \mathbf{\iota}_X.m + \mathbf{\iota}_Y.m + \dots + \mathbf{\iota}_Z.m.
\]
When this condition is satisfied for every element of $M$, it is so for $N$
and $Q$ as well.

The preceding lemma allows us to define a new \emph{abelian category}
$\mathbf{A}$:

\begin{itemize}
\item The objects of $\mathbf{A}$ are the left modules $M$ over
      $\mathbf{Z}[C]$ such that $\mathbf{Z}[C].M$ is equal to $M$, and whose
      underlying set belongs to $\Univ$.

\item If $M$ and $N$ are two objects of $\mathbf{A}$,
      $\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(M,N)$ is the set of
      $\mathbf{Z}[C]$-linear maps of $M$ into $N$; the composition of the
      morphisms is done in the usual way.
\end{itemize}

In particular, the left module underlying $\mathbf{Z}[C]$ and the left ideals
$\mathbf{Z}[C].\mathbf{\iota}_X$ are objects of $\mathbf{A}$. A morphism
$\varphi$ of $\mathbf{Z}[C].\mathbf{\iota}_X$ into an object $M$ of
$\mathbf{A}$ is determined by the datum of the element
$\varphi(\mathbf{\iota}_X)$ of $\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$. The abelian group
$\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{Z}[C].\mathbf{\iota}_X,M)$ is therefore
identified with $\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$, and
$\mathbf{Z}[C].\mathbf{\iota}_X$ is a projective object of $\mathbf{A}$. It is
the same for $\mathbf{Z}[C]$, which is the direct sum of the
$\mathbf{Z}[C].\mathbf{\iota}_X$. Hence:

\begin{lemma}
The left module underlying $\mathbf{Z}[C]$ is a projective generator of
$\mathbf{A}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proposition}
The categories $\mathbf{A}$ and $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$
are equivalent.
\end{proposition}

We are going to construct functors
\[
  T\colon \mathbf{A}\longrightarrow
  \operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})
  \qquad\text{and}\qquad
  S\colon \operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})\longrightarrow\mathbf{A}
\]
such that $S\circ T$ and $T\circ S$ are isomorphic respectively to the
identity maps of $\mathbf{A}$ and of $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$.

For this purpose let $M$ be an object of $\mathbf{A}$. For every object $X$ of
$\mathbf{C}$, we denote by $(TM)X$ the abelian group
$\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$. For every morphism $f\colon X\to Y$, we denote by
$(TM)(f)$ the $\mathbf{Z}$-linear map of $\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$ into
$\mathbf{\iota}_Y(M)$ which is induced by the homothety of $M$ defined by
$f$. We have thus defined a functor $TM$ of $\mathbf{C}$ into $\mathbf{Ab}$.
Moreover, if $\varphi\colon M\to N$ is a $\mathbf{Z}[C]$-linear map,
$\varphi$ maps $\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$ into $\mathbf{\iota}_X(N)$. We shall denote
by $(T\varphi)X$ the map of $\mathbf{\iota}_X(M)$ into
$\mathbf{\iota}_X(N)$ thus defined. As $X$ ranges over the objects of
$\mathbf{C}$, the maps $(T\varphi)X$ obviously define a natural
transformation of $TM$ into $TN$. The verification of the following lemma is
left to the reader:

\begin{lemma}
The maps $M\mapsto TM$, $\varphi\mapsto T\varphi$ define a functor $T$ of
the category $\mathbf{A}$ into $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$.
\end{lemma}

Conversely, let $F$ be an object of
$\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$. If $f\colon X\to Y$ is a
morphism of $\mathbf{C}$ and $m = (m_X)$ an element of
$\sum_{X\in\mathbf{OC}}FX$, we define a product $f.m$ by means of the formula
\[
  \text{projection of } f.m \text{ onto } FZ
  \;=\;
  \begin{cases}
    0            & \text{if } Z \neq Y,\\
    (Ff)(m_X)    & \text{if } Z = Y.
  \end{cases}
\]

The direct sum $\sum_{X\in\mathbf{OC}}FX$ is thus equipped with a
left module structure over the ring $\mathbf{Z}[C]$. We shall denote by
$SF$ this module, which is evidently an object of $\mathbf{A}$.
Likewise, if $\psi\colon F\to G$ is a natural transformation, we shall
denote by $S\psi$ the linear map
\[
  \sum_X\psi(X)\colon \sum_X FX\to \sum_X GX.
\]

\begin{lemma}
The maps $F\mapsto SF$, $\psi\mapsto S\psi$ define a functor $S$ from
$\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$ to $\mathbf{A}$.
\end{lemma}

It is clear that $T$ and $S$ satisfy the required conditions. This
completes the proof of Proposition~2. Lemma~2 therefore implies the
following proposition:

\begin{proposition}
The category $\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$ has a
projective generator.
\end{proposition}

Indeed, $\mathbf{Z}[C]$ is a projective generator of $\mathbf{A}$. It
follows that $T\mathbf{Z}[C]$ is a projective generator of
$\operatorname{Fonct}(\mathbf{C},\mathbf{Ab})$. Moreover, this functor is
none other than the functor
$Y\mapsto \sum_{X\in\mathbf{OC}}\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathbf{C}}(X,Y)$.
