\section{Goldie's Theorem \textup{\cite{goldie}, \cite{lesieur-croisot-1}}}

--- This paragraph is devoted to a theorem of Goldie which we shall
prove by our methods. Let $A$ be a ring and let $F$ be the set of right
ideals of $A$ of which $A_d$ is an essential extension. For a right
ideal $I$ to belong to $F$, it is necessary and sufficient that the
following condition hold: for every nonzero element $a$ of $A$, there
exists $b\in A$ such that $a.b$ is not zero and belongs to $I$. It
easily follows from this condition that the set $F$ is topologising. We
can therefore speak of the largest $F$-negligible right ideal; this
ideal is a characteristic submodule of $A_d$, that is, a two-sided ideal
of $A$. In this paragraph, we suppose that this two-sided ideal is
zero; in other words, we suppose that the following assertion is true:

$(\star)$ \emph{If $A$ is an essential extension of the right ideal $I$
and if $x.I$ is zero, $x\in A$, then $x$ is zero.}

\begin{lemma}
If condition $(\star)$ is satisfied, the set $F$ of right ideals of
which $A_d$ is an essential extension is topologising and idempotent.
\end{lemma}

Let us show that $F$ is idempotent: let $\mathfrak{m}$ be an element of
$F.F$ and $a$ a nonzero element of $A$; there exists an element $I$ of
$F$ such that $(a.I+\mathfrak{m})/\mathfrak{m}$ is $F$-negligible
(cf.\ \S2). By $(\star)$, the product $a.I$ is not zero. There is
therefore an $l\in I$ such that $a.l$ is not zero and such that $a.l.n$
is contained in $\mathfrak{m}$ for at least one $n\in F$. This shows
that $\mathfrak{m}$ already belongs to $F$.

The set $F$ therefore defines a localisation. Condition $(\star)$
implies that the canonical map from $A$ into $A_F$ is injective.
Moreover, one has the lemma:

\begin{lemma}
$A_F$ is an injective $A$-module.
\end{lemma}

Let indeed $f:I\to A_F$ be an $A$-linear map from a right ideal of $A$
into $A_F$. If $I$ is an element of $F$, $f$ evidently extends to $A$
(Chapter~III, \S2). If $I$ is an arbitrary right ideal, let
$\mathfrak{m}$ be a complement of $I$ in $A_d$ (Chapter~II, \S5); then
$I+\mathfrak{m}$ is isomorphic to the direct sum $I\oplus\mathfrak{m}$
and $f$ extends to an $A$-linear map $g:I+\mathfrak{m}\to A_F$. Since
$A$ is an essential extension of $I+\mathfrak{m}$, $I+\mathfrak{m}$
belongs to $F$ and $g$ extends to $A$.

\begin{lemma}
Every $\mathbf{F}$-closed $A$-module $M$ contained in $A_F$ is a direct
factor of $A_F$.
\end{lemma}

Let us identify $A$ with its image in $A_F$ and let $N$ be a complement
of $M\cap A$ in $A$. The module $A_d$ is then an essential extension of
$M\cap A+N$, and the quotient $A_F/(M+N)$ is $F$-negligible. It follows
that the canonical injection from $M+N$ into $A_F$ is an
$\mathbf{F}$-envelope. The lemma therefore follows from the formula
\[
A_F=(M\oplus N)_F=M_F\oplus N_F=M\oplus N_F.
\]

\begin{lemma}
The ring $A_F$ is regular in the sense of von
Neumann \textup{(\cite{bourbaki-algebre}, \S6, exercise~15)} (one also
says ``absolutely flat'' instead of regular, \textup{\cite{bourbaki-commutative},
Chapter~I, \S2, exercises)}.
\end{lemma}

We want to show that every monogenic ideal $a.A_F$ is generated by an
idempotent. For this, we denote by $f_a$ the endomorphism $b\mapsto a.b$
of $A_F$. The map $a\mapsto f_a$ is an isomorphism from the ring $A_F$
onto the ring of endomorphisms of the $A$-module $A_F$. Since $A_F$ is
$\mathbf{F}$-closed, $\operatorname{Ker}f_a$ is $\mathbf{F}$-closed and
is a direct factor of $A_F$ (Lemma~3). If $M$ is a complement of
$\operatorname{Ker}f_a$, $M$ is injective (Lemma~2) and $f_a$ induces an
isomorphism from $M$ onto a direct factor of $A_F$. Lemma~4 therefore
follows from Lemma~5:

\begin{lemma}
Let $A$ be a ring, $M$ an $A$-module and $B$ the ring of endomorphisms
of $M$. If $b$ is an element of $B$, the following assertions are
equivalent:
\begin{description}
\item[\normalfont a.] The right ideal $b.B$ is a direct factor of $B_d$.
\item[\normalfont b.] The left ideal $B.b$ is a direct factor of
${}_{s}B$.
\item[\normalfont c.] $\operatorname{Ker}b$ and $\operatorname{Im}b$ are
direct factors of $M$.
\end{description}
\end{lemma}

The proof of this lemma is obviously left to the reader.

The corollary to Proposition~3 shows that $A_F$ is also an injective
$A_F$-module. One shall remark moreover that, with the notations of
paragraph~2, $F'$ is none other than the set of right ideals of $A_F$
of which $A_F$ is an essential extension. Moreover, the ring $A_F$ also
satisfies $(\star)$.

We sum up the preceding lemmas in

\begin{theorem}
If the ring $A$ satisfies condition $(\star)$, the set of right ideals
of which $A_d$ is an essential extension is topologising and
idempotent. The ring $A_F$ is regular and the right $A_F$-module
underlying $A_F$ is injective. As an $A$-module, $A_F$ is the injective
envelope of $A_d$.
\end{theorem}

Let us cite two sets of applications of Theorem~1:

a. Let $A$ be a ring of which every nonzero right ideal contains a
nonzero idempotent. It amounts to the same to say that for every nonzero
element $a$ of $A$, there exists a nonzero element $x\in A$ such that
$x.a.x=x$ (this holds if $A$ is a regular ring in the sense of von
Neumann). If $I$ is a right ideal of $A$ and if $x.I$ is zero, $I$
annihilates every left ideal of the form $A.e$, where $e$ runs through
the idempotents contained in $A.x$. If $x$ is not zero, one can choose
$e$ different from $0$. Then $I$ is contained in $(1-e).A$ and $A_d$
cannot be an essential extension of $I$. This proves condition $(\star)$.

One sees in particular that every regular ring in the sense of von
Neumann embeds into a regular ``right injective'' ring.

b. Every integral domain and every quasi-simple ring (there is no
nonzero proper two-sided ideal) embed into a regular ring. We shall see
that, under noetherian conditions, this regular ring is a simple ring.

\begin{lemma}
If the ring $A$ satisfies condition $(\star)$, the following assertions
are equivalent:
\begin{description}
\item[\normalfont a.] The ring $A_F$ is semisimple.
\item[\normalfont b.] There is no infinite family formed of right ideals
of $A$ whose sum is direct.
\end{description}
\end{lemma}

$(a)\Rightarrow(b)$: for let $n$ be the length of the $A_F$-module
underlying $A_F$. Let $(I_i)_{i\in I}$ be a family of right ideals of
$A$ whose sum is direct. The formula
\[
\left(\sum_{i\in I}I_i\right)_{F}=\sum_{i\in I}(I_i)_{F}
\]
shows that the number of elements of $I$ is less than $n$.

$(b)\Rightarrow(a)$: if assertion~(b) is true, a classical argument
proves the existence of a finite family $(I_i)_{i\in I}$ formed of right
ideals of $A$ and satisfying the following conditions: the sum of the
$I_i$ is direct and $A_d$ is an essential extension of this sum;
moreover, $I_i$ is a coirreducible $A$-module (cf.\ Chapter~II, \S5; in
general, it is equivalent to say that the injective envelope of an
$A$-module $M$ is a direct sum of a finite family of indecomposable
injectives, or to say that there is no infinite family of submodules of
$M$ whose sum is direct). It follows that the right ideals
$(I_i)_{F}$ are indecomposable and that their sum is direct and equal
to $A_F$; the regular ring $A_F$ is therefore a direct sum of a finite
family of indecomposable ideals. This proves~(a).

\begin{lemma}
Let $A$ be a ring satisfying the following conditions:
\begin{description}
\item[\normalfont a.] Every nilpotent ideal of $A$ is zero.
\item[\normalfont b.] The right ideals of the form
$(0:a)=\{x\mid x\in A,\ a.x=0\}$ satisfy the ascending chain condition.
\end{description}
The ring $A$ then satisfies condition $(\star)$.
\end{lemma}

Let $a$ be a nonzero element of $A$, and let us prove that $A_d$ is not
an essential extension of $(0:a)$. For this, we choose an element
$c\in a.A$ such that $(0:c)$ is maximal among the ideals of the form
$(0:x)$, where $x\in a.A$ and $x\ne 0$. Since $c.A$ is not nilpotent,
there exists an element $d\in A$ such that $a.d.c$ is different from $0$.
There then follow from the inclusions
\[
(0:c)\subset(0:d.c)\subset(0:a.d.c)
\]
and from the maximal character of $(0:c)$ that $(0:d.c)$ is equal to
$(0:a.d.c)$. In other words, the equality $a.d.c.x=0$ implies the
equality $d.c.x=0$. This shows that the intersection
$d.c.A\cap(0:a)$ is zero and that $A_d$ is not an essential extension
of $(0:a)$.

\begin{theorem}[Goldie]
Let $A$ be a ring satisfying the following conditions:
\begin{description}
\item[\normalfont a.] Every nilpotent ideal of $A$ is zero.
\item[\normalfont b.] The right ideals of the form
$(0:a)=\{x\mid x\in A,\ a.x=0\}$ satisfy the ascending chain condition.
\item[\normalfont c.] There is no infinite family formed of right ideals
whose sum is direct.
\end{description}
The ring $A$ satisfies condition $(\star)$ and the localisation $A_F$
of $A$ is a semisimple ring. If $S$ is the multiplicative set formed of
the regular elements of $A$, the pair $(A_F,u_A)$ is a right ring of
fractions of $A$ for $S$.
\end{theorem}

It remains to prove the last assertion: if $s\in A$ is regular on the
left (i.e.\ $s.x=0$ implies $x=0$), the homothety $x\mapsto s.x$ defines
injective endomorphisms of $A$ and of $A_F$. This homothety is therefore
an automorphism of $A_F$ and $s$ is invertible in $A_F$; in particular,
$s$ is regular on the right in $A$. The multiplicative set $S$ is
therefore formed of the elements regular on the left in $A$. For the
proof to be complete, it suffices to show that, with the notations of
paragraph~2, $F$ is equal to $F_S$ [Proposition~5,~$(c)$]. For this, we
prove that a right ideal $I$ belongs to $F$ if and only if $I$ contains
an $s\in S$:

If $s$ is a regular element of $A$, the ideal $s.A_F$ is equal to
$A_F$. It follows that $s.A$ belongs to $F$.

Conversely, let $I$ be an element of $F$. We shall construct two
sequences $(e_1,\dots,e_n)$ and $(a_1,\dots,a_n)$, formed of elements of
$A_F$ and satisfying the following conditions: for every $i$, $e_i$ is
a primitive idempotent of $A_F$; $A_F$ is the direct sum of the
indecomposable ideals $e_i.A_F$; for every $i$, $e_i.a_i$ is not zero
and belongs to $I$; if $f_i$ denotes the homothety
$x\mapsto e_i.a_i.x$, one has the equality
$\operatorname{Ker}f_1\cap\dots\cap\operatorname{Ker}f_n=0$. If we put
$s=e_1.a_1+\dots+e_n.a_n$, it will follow from these conditions that the
image of $A_F$ under the homothety $x\mapsto s.x$ meets every ideal
$e_i.A_F$ (for $s.x$ belongs to $e_i.A_F$ if $x$ belongs to the
intersection of the $\operatorname{Ker}f_j$ for $j\ne i$); this image
being $\mathbf{F}$-closed, it will coincide with $A_F$ and the
homothety will be bijective. The element $s$ will therefore be regular
and will belong to $I$.

Let us take for $e_1$ an arbitrary primitive idempotent of $A_F$. Let
$\mathfrak{m}_1$ be an element of $F$ such that $e_1.\mathfrak{m}_1$ is
contained in $I$. Since $e_1.\mathfrak{m}_1$ is not nilpotent, there
exists $a_1\in\mathfrak{m}_1$ such that $e_1.a_1.e_1$ is not zero. One
deduces the formula
\[
A_F=e_1.A_F\oplus\operatorname{Ker}f_1.
\]

Let us take for $e_2$ a primitive idempotent belonging to
$\operatorname{Ker}f_1$. Let $\mathfrak{m}_2$ be an element of $F$ such
that $e_2.\mathfrak{m}_2$ is contained in $I$. Since
$e_2.\mathfrak{m}_2$ is not nilpotent, there exists
$a_2\in\mathfrak{m}_2$ such that $e_2.a_2.e_2$ is not zero. One deduces
the formula
\[
A_F=e_1.A_F\oplus e_2.A_F\oplus
\operatorname{Ker}f_1\cap\operatorname{Ker}f_2.
\]

Let us take for $e_3$ a primitive idempotent belonging to
$\operatorname{Ker}f_1\cap\operatorname{Ker}f_2$, \dots. The construction
stops when $n$ is equal to the length of the right $A_F$-module
underlying $A_F$. One then has the formulas
\[
A_F=e_1.A_F+\dots+e_n.A_F\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\operatorname{Ker}f_1\cap\dots\cap\operatorname{Ker}f_n=0.
\]

\begin{corollary}
Let $A$ be a ring satisfying conditions~(b) and~(c) of Theorem~2 and of
which $0$ is a prime two-sided ideal (i.e.\ if $\mathfrak{a}$ and
$\mathfrak{b}$ are two nonzero two-sided ideals of $A$, the product
$\mathfrak{a}.\mathfrak{b}$ is not zero). If $S$ is the multiplicative
set formed of the regular elements of $A$, there exists a right ring of
fractions of $A$ for $S$. This ring of fractions is simple.
\end{corollary}

Condition~(a) of Theorem~2 is indeed satisfied. If $A_F$ were not
simple, $A_F$ would contain nonzero two-sided ideals $\mathfrak{a}$
and $\mathfrak{b}$ whose product is zero. The intersections
$\mathfrak{a}\cap A$ and $\mathfrak{b}\cap A$ would then be two-sided
ideals of $A$ and the product of these ideals would be zero. This is
absurd.

\begin{corollary}
Let $A$ be a right noetherian integral domain. If $S$ is the
multiplicative set formed of the nonzero elements of $A$, there exists
a right ring of fractions of $A$ for $S$. This ring is a skew field.
\end{corollary}

The existence of the ring of fractions $A_S$ follows from Theorem~2. If
$A_S$ is not a skew field, $A_S$ contains an idempotent $e$ distinct
from $1$. There is therefore an $s\in S$ such that $e.s$ belongs to $A$.
Since $e.s$ is not invertible in $A_S$, the $x\in A_S$ such that
$e.s.x$ is zero form a right ideal $I$. It follows that $A\cap I$ is
not zero and is annihilated by $e.s$: this is absurd.
